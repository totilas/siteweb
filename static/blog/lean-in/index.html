Results automatically formalized: time to Lean in?

Edwige Cyffers

I was exposed to proof assistants quite early. Gender bias at work: I knew nothing about computer science before starting my studies, and I first learned algorithms through pseudo-code. In 2017, when I entered ENS Lyon and joined the CS department, I had to learn how to write code quite quickly. The very same year, I took a course on proof assistants and had to write proofs in Coq (since then renamed Rocq, for obvious reasons). I loved the concept, but this first experience was awful. I distinctly remember wasting my Saturday night trying to formally prove that the successor of an odd number is even, and similarly obvious facts. The software crashed whenever I made a syntax mistake (e.g. forgetting the dot at the end of a statement). I saw no future there, and gave up.

Instead of being sure to be correct while accomplishing nothing, I moved to machine learning, where you are never sure but somehow it works most of the time. Of course, there is a long history of attempts to bridge symbolic AI and machine learning, two communities that could get the best of both worlds but usually fail to find a useful way to combine them, so formal proofs remained a distant dream. I was still curious about them, and in 2020 I even accepted a paid job that turned out to be translating LaTeX into Lean. I came out of this second experience even more suspicious of the field: Lean was broken a bit everywhere at the time, so any RL system would first find a bug, prove False, and then deduce the theorem from it. So once again, I put it back on the shelf of nice ideas that, so far, fail when you try to implement them.

Fortunately, more ambitious people kept believing in the idea and made it work. It turns out that even if writing proofs in Lean is still mostly unbearable for the average human being, LLMs are fluent in this language as well. So this late spring, using them to check my own math results became a real temptation.

Thanks to a first generous anonymous donor, I started formalizing one of my old papers. The LLM came back with many extremely precise questions: it was the best review I have had in my life. While co-authors usually understand the main ideas but keep ignoring some small details, the LLM was to the point, understood the technical difficulties, and even found real inaccuracies (which I was aware of, but which no reviewer had ever pointed out). At that point, I decided that Lean was a good skill, i.e. a good way to force the LLM not to be lazy and to actually produce good mathematical discussions.

Unfortunately, at that point the first generous LLM access was revoked, and the experiment stopped (yes, I have not been selected for any of the academic LLM programs I have applied to so far, and I have no way to pay for a Max subscription with my research funding). However, at the same time, I was working on a paper with Juba Ziani whose proofs were mostly self-contained, simple and rather elegant: they were calling for a Lean formalization. Fable was released, and I had access to enough tokens to give it a first shot. Not enough to finish, but the structure was there, and I was fortunate to find a second generous anonymous donor. Shortly after, Opus 5.5 was released and finished the work effortlessly (i.e. hitting my token limits only a few times). So I decided to share this experience, in the hope that someone finally gives me a free subscription it can help other researchers who would like to give it a try but do not know how to start, and imagine, out of ignorance, that it is complicated.

I don’t know which works are the most interesting to check. My guess is that if you want to reach out to the Lean community, the best place is Zulip: https://leanprover.zulipchat.com. Some references that seem hard to avoid:

  • the Fermat’s Last Theorem project (ongoing): https://imperialcollegelondon.github.io/FLT/
  • the Liquid Tensor Experiment (2021–2022), which checked a central theorem of Clausen–Scholze condensed mathematics at Scholze’s request;
  • the Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture (Gowers, Green, Manners, Tao), whose proof was formalized in about three weeks after the preprint appeared: https://teorth.github.io/pfr/
  • OpenAI’s massive dump: https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/overview.pdf
  • a critical analysis of the Lean proof of the Navier–Stokes result, showing that two of its lemmas do not match the written proof: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08144
  • Kevin Buzzard’s post on what “the proof is checked in Lean” should mean, and what to check before believing it: https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/11/12/and-the-proof-is-checked-in-lean/
  • a Lean formalization of the deductive structure of a claimed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, which its README states is conditional on an unproved input: https://github.com/startigerjln/Nielsen-and-Semita-Riemann-Proof-in-Lean4/.

Before starting

Lean takes a lot of space, and I don’t fully understand why. So delete a few old movies from your computer if you don’t want to experience the bad scenario of your LLM filling up your disk and getting stuck because nothing can move anymore. Apart from that, installing Lean is quite easy, and your LLM, knowing your configuration, will likely be more helpful than I am.

It is important to understand that Lean relies on the trick that a proof is exactly a program, which means that the proof is correct if the Lean code compiles and contains no sorry (the placeholder for a missing proof, which Lean accepts with a mere warning). The proof is written in .lean files. After compilation, you get .olean files. To avoid re-checking all the maths, you can use the cache of the proofs already compiled in the library. In other words, running

lake exe cache get

makes it much faster. Lake is Lean’s build tool and package manager. VS Code has an extension (its symbol is ∀) that starts a Lean server and shows you whether the proof is correct or not. Overall, this interface has worked well for me so far, except that it can take time to run. Luckily, most of the interactions were done directly by the LLM.

Formalizing my paper

Choosing the paper

During my semester at the Simons Institute, I was puzzled by how many smart people were working at the intersection of privacy and data markets, often using heterogeneous privacy budgets. I was not at all convinced by the idea, but a gut feeling is not a math proof, and I did not know where to start to test whether it was a bad idea or not. Over our discussions, it became clear to me that what matters is to pick a reasonable epsilon that covers a good proportion of the use cases, but it was still not quite there. Luckily, Juba was convinced that it would be a fun project.

This ended up as this fun paper (the code is on GitHub), which compares the best affine estimator to a single-threshold estimator. In short, the affine estimator takes advantage of all the distinct privacy budgets to suck as much as possible out of the data, whereas the single-threshold operator picks the smartest epsilon, gives that level to all the people who are satisfied with it, and ignores the rest of the users. This was a good candidate for a Lean proof for several reasons:

  • The theorems actually matter. That was the first objection I got from a researcher I really like: are you doing maths serious enough for this to be worth it? I looked offended, but I was not. I agree that most of my theorems are pretty much useless: their goal is mostly to introduce precisely enough the phenomena and algorithms I develop, and if some hypotheses are missing or some constant gets lost in a convergence rate that does not seem tight anyway, I don’t really think it makes a difference. Some of my theorems are indeed there for illustrative purposes. In this paper, in contrast, the exact values of the constants are interesting. On the other hand, the proof techniques are not that advanced or interesting, so skipping the proofs does not significantly hinder understanding. Or at least, I can see how the results can be interesting for people who cannot read the proofs.
  • The math is easy. Otherwise, it would not be a problem for the LLM, but it would be for me. In my previous experiment, I ended up in a technical discussion about adaptivity in DP proofs, and as I mainly use adaptivity results in DP as a black box, I was not sure what to answer the LLM.

Prompting the LLM

This part is likely to change. As is clear from the introduction, I did not use a single prompt, and I don’t have my prompts anymore, so I went in reverse and asked Claude for its best prompt given what we have, which I copy verbatim, including what to do next. It is likely a better prompt than what I did, but the key points remain:

  • no sorry
  • actually run Lean, don’t just hallucinate whether it works or not
  • give me a clear correspondence with the paper.
Show the full prompt (46 lines)
Formalize the paper in <path to PDF/tex> in Lean 4 + Mathlib, in a new Lake project
under lean/ (Mathlib version: <pin, or latest stable>).

Goal: every formal claim of the paper (theorems, propositions, lemmas, corollaries,
and the mathematical content of remarks and examples) has a Lean counterpart.

Statements
- Each Lean statement must be at least as strong as the paper's claim: same
  conclusion, no extra hypotheses. Where the paper leaves a hypothesis implicit
  (measurability, integrability, nonemptiness, n ≥ 1…), state it explicitly and
  list it in the README.
- Proofs may differ from the paper's.
- When a result is about concrete objects (mechanisms, estimators, algorithms),
  prove it for those objects, not only for an abstract formula. Link the two
  explicitly (e.g. "the MSE of this mechanism equals this formula").
- Prefer generic statements when the paper reuses an argument (state it once with
  parameters, then instantiate).

Definitions
- Reuse Mathlib whenever it has the notion. Before defining something yourself,
  search Mathlib and the main Lean libraries in the area, and tell me what exists.
- For each definition written from scratch, show it next to the paper's
  definition. Where an independent formalization exists, propose a bridge lemma
  proving the two agree.
- Name variables so they don't suggest a different mathematical object than the
  paper's (e.g. a law vs a random variable).

Structure
- One file per section of the paper, in the paper's order. Docstrings cite the
  paper's PDF numbering (Theorem 2, Lemma 3…), never LaTeX labels.

Quality bar
- No sorry, no new axioms. `#print axioms` on the main theorems shows only
  propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound.
- `lake build` passes with no errors. Actually run Lean; don't reason about
  whether the code would compile.
- Don't stop until every claim is covered or explicitly listed as not covered,
  with the reason.

Deliverables
- README: a disclaimer (the paper's correctness rests on its written proofs; this
  is a complementary machine check), scope, build instructions, and per-file
  tables "Paper (PDF numbering) | Lean name".
- A final report: what is covered, what isn't, every place where the Lean statement
  differs from the paper (stronger, more general, extra explicit hypothesis), and
  any errors found in the paper.
Claude also added three practical notes
  • Attach the PDF as well as the tex, so the numbering matches what readers see.
  • Ask for the Mathlib/library survey first, before any code. That is when decisions like SampCert vs from scratch should be made, not afterwards.
  • For a long paper, split the work by section across sessions, and run a final pass that checks the README tables against the paper.

Going through the definitions

From here on, it is hard to stay generic, and as I have nothing against advertising my own research, you might become familiar with my paper if you keep reading! The structure of the paper is quite simple. It recalls the usual definition of heterogeneous DP and the mean squared error we want to minimize, defines the baseline affine operator and how it simplifies in some cases, introduces our estimator, and then proves that our estimator is quite good compared to the baseline. Then we show some illustrative theorems to argue that the finding matters, as it transfers from mean estimation to DP-SGD.

The Lean structure thus mimics the paper’s structure. Here is Claude’s diagram of it: the formalization has about 5,000 lines in twelve files, organized in four layers.

I also asked Claude to provide, in the README, a table of correspondence between the results of the paper and their Lean counterparts. For someone fluent in Lean, I guess this is useful, as it makes it possible to spot-check some of the equivalences and to know with high probability whether what is in the repo is valid.

The first definition we can check is the DP one. Here are the paper and Lean versions side by side. In the paper (Definition 4), each record i has its own budget \varepsilon^{(i)}, and M is heterogeneous \varepsilon-DP if for every i,

\Pr(M(D) \in S) \le e^{\varepsilon^{(i)}} \Pr(M(D'^{\,i}) \in S)

for all measurable sets S and all datasets D, D'^{\,i} that differ arbitrarily only in the i-th component.

Here is the Lean version, exactly as in Privacy/Defs.lean:

def NeighborAt {n : ℕ} {X : Type*} (i : Fin n) (D D' : Fin n → X) : Prop :=
  ∀ j, j ≠ i → D j = D' j

def HetDP {n : ℕ} {X Y : Type*} [MeasurableSpace Y]
    (PM : (Fin n → X) → Measure Y) (ε : Fin n → ℝ) : Prop :=
  ∀ i D D', NeighborAt i D D' → ∀ S, MeasurableSet S →
    PM D S ≤ ENNReal.ofReal (Real.exp (ε i)) * PM D' S

I am assuming that the keywords def and := are self-explanatory. After the name comes a list of arguments: those between curly braces are implicit (they are inferred when you use the definition), and those between parentheses are the ones you must provide. For the adjacency relation, you thus have the index and two databases, and it states the logical property that all other entries remain identical. Lean is typed, so we can see the type of each argument: ℕ is straightforward, Fin n is the set of integers smaller than n, and Type* can be whatever type we want. As in most functional languages, juxtaposition is function application, which is why you do not need parentheses everywhere: PM D S corresponds to (PM D) S, i.e. PM(D)(S), and parentheses are only used when you need to delimit the arguments of a function. Functions from a given module are called as in Python, e.g. Real.exp, so this is easy to follow as well.

The second definition is a bit more complex, because it uses Mathlib, the library containing well-defined mathematics. In particular, we have [MeasurableSpace Y], a clever way Lean handles polymorphism. We know from the definition that the output space Y is a measurable space, but not which one: Lean selects the right σ-algebra from the context, so the same definition applies to the Borel sets of \mathbb{R} as well as to any weird measurable space that would appear. Here I was a bit confused by Claude’s first translation. As you can see, the definition is not the same as the LaTeX one: rather than reasoning about the probability of M(D), the formalization directly uses the output laws, not the random variables. This was something I did not catch from the conversation, and that I discovered by looking at the definition. It sounds like a reasonable choice, so the only modification I made was to ask for it to be renamed PM: Claude’s initial definition called it M, which meant that M did not have the same type in the LaTeX and in the Lean, which was problematic. The rest of the definition is pretty clear: ENNReal.ofReal is needed for the explicit conversion between reals and extended non-negative reals, the space where measures take their values.

From this, we construct the affine operators. As defined in Section III-B, the affine operator releases \hat\mu_w(D) = \sum_i w_i x_i + Z with Z \sim \mathrm{Lap}(\eta), for records x_i \in [-1/2, 1/2], and heterogeneous \varepsilon-DP forces \eta \ge \max_i w_i/\varepsilon^{(i)}. In Lean:

noncomputable def laplacePDFReal (b x : ℝ) : ℝ := Real.exp (-|x| / b) / (2 * b)

noncomputable def laplacePDF (b x : ℝ) : ℝ≥0∞ := ENNReal.ofReal (laplacePDFReal b x)

A few novelties are needed to keep reading the Lean definitions. First, a function is written fun z => …. The noncomputable keyword is a bit out of my reach: it means that this cannot be turned into executable code, here because real numbers and measures are not objects Lean can compute with. It does not change what is proved. In ML, no one cares whether things are constructive or not, so no one cares about using noncomputable. But someone formalizing a paper about logic would need to be more careful, and would look at the axioms each proof relies on (#print axioms), which is where non-constructive reasoning shows up. We define a record Rec not with def but with abbrev, to shorten the proofs: it means that the definition is always replaced directly, rather than having to be unpacked. Finally, we can state under which condition the affine estimator is heterogeneous DP. Here I only report the statement of the theorem: what follows by is the proof itself, hidden in the dots.

As we study the mean squared error in the paper, we also have a definition for it, which introduces other notations, so I report it as well.

noncomputable def mse (PM : (Fin n → ℝ) → Measure ℝ) (P : Measure ℝ) : ℝ :=
  ∫ x, ∫ y, (y - ∫ t, t ∂P) ^ 2 ∂(PM x) ∂(Measure.pi fun _ : Fin n => P)

noncomputable def drMSE (PM : (Fin n → ℝ) → Measure ℝ) : ℝ :=
  sSup (mse PM '' SupportedProb)

Here you have to remember the unusual choice to reason about the law of the mechanism M rather than random variables. The outer integral ∫ x, … ∂(Measure.pi fun _ : Fin n => P) takes the expectation with respect to P^n, the distribution of n i.i.d. samples from P. Then ∫ y, … ∂(PM x) is the integral over the output of M on the input x. In other words, the Lean definition is indeed

\mathrm{MSE} = \int \left( \int (y - \mu_P)^2 \, d(\mathrm{PM}\ x)(y) \right) dP^n(x).

Then we still need to take the supremum over all possible distributions. A weird notation is '': here mse takes as second argument a measure rather than a set of measures, so we need to lift the function to sets, which we do carelessly in maths, but which changes the type, so it has to be done explicitly in Lean.

Going deeper

From this, we can move to the definitions of the best affine estimator and of the threshold operator. Most of the useful definitions for this are in Risks.lean and MeanEstimation.lean. Allow me to skip some of them and keep only the most interesting ones.

Show the Lean code (31 lines)
noncomputable def persObj (A B : ℝ) (w : ι → ℝ) (M : ℝ) : ℝ :=
  A * ∑ i, w i ^ 2 + B * M ^ 2

noncomputable def nThr (pub : Finset ι) (u : ι → ℝ) (t : ℝ) : ℕ :=
  #{i | i ∈ pub ∨ t ≤ u i}

def persSet (pub : Finset ι) (u : ι → ℝ) (A B : ℝ) : Set ℝ :=
  {r | ∃ w M, Feasible pub u w M ∧ persObj A B w M = r}

/-- Values of the threshold objective over admissible thresholds. -/
def thrSet (pub : Finset ι) (u : ι → ℝ) (A B : ℝ) : Set ℝ :=
  {r | ∃ t, 0 < t ∧ 0 < nThr pub u t ∧ thrObj A B t (nThr pub u t) = r}

/-- `𝓡_pers(u; A, B)`, Eq. `eq:generic-pers`. -/
noncomputable def persRisk (pub : Finset ι) (u : ι → ℝ) (A B : ℝ) : ℝ :=
  sInf (persSet pub u A B)

/-- `𝓡_thr(u; A, B)`, Eq. `eq:generic-thr`. -/
noncomputable def thrRisk (pub : Finset ι) (u : ι → ℝ) (A B : ℝ) : ℝ :=
  sInf (thrSet pub u A B)

noncomputable def thrMechPub (pub : Finset (Fin n)) (ε : Fin n → ℝ) (t : ℝ)
    (x : Fin n → ℝ) : Measure ℝ :=
  (laplace (1 / ((nThr pub ε t : ℝ) * t))).map
    (fun z => 1 / (nThr pub ε t : ℝ) *
      ∑ i, (if i ∈ pub ∨ t ≤ ε i then (1 : ℝ) else 0) * x i + z)

def affRiskSetPub (pub : Finset (Fin n)) (ε : Fin n → ℝ) : Set ℝ :=
  {r | ∃ (w : Fin n → ℝ) (η : ℝ), (∀ i, 0 ≤ w i) ∧ ∑ i, w i = 1 ∧ 0 < η ∧
    HetDPPub pub (fun D : Fin n → Rec => affineMech w η (fun i => (D i : ℝ))) ε ∧
    drMSE (affineMech w η) = r}

Once we have all of this, the theorems themselves are easy to read. Here is the first one:

theorem public_mse_factor_two (pub : Finset (Fin n)) (ε : Fin n → ℝ) (ε₁ : ℝ)
    (hε₁ : 0 < ε₁) (hpub : pub.Nonempty) (hpriv : pubᶜ.Nonempty)
    (hu : ∀ i ∉ pub, ε i = ε₁) :
    sInf {r | ∃ t, 0 < t ∧ 0 < nThr pub ε t ∧ drMSE (thrMechPub pub ε t) = r}
      ≤ 2 * sInf (affRiskSetPub pub ε)

As you can see, it states that the ratio between the single-threshold and the best affine estimators, with public data and one private privacy level, is at most two. It is very similar for the general case, showing that the ratio is at most \min\{(1 + \log_2 n)^2, m^2\} for m different privacy levels, and that it can indeed grow like m^2, which is attained when each privacy budget is double the previous one but with half as much data.

Then there is the extension to gradient descent, which I also reviewed chunk by chunk in the chat. I have the impression that it was a lot of work for little mathematical content, as it requires building probabilities in higher dimensions and doing well-known optimization, which are a bit orthogonal to the paper.

Note that I have not looked at the proofs at all. It is indeed possible that they differ from the paper’s. Here I would argue that Occam’s razor is enough for me. The proofs are not particularly difficult or novel: the LaTeX version is thus a plausible path to a proof that compiles, and it is unlikely that ignoring the “oracle” of the LaTeX proof would cost fewer tokens. I would also assume that Claude would have flagged it, and finally, I don’t believe that the exact correspondence between the Lean proofs and the paper’s matters in this case. How to do it in general remains quite open, even if I have the impression that intermediate lemmas and definitions channel the information and limit the risks. I would be happy to test or discuss ways to harness the LLM more precisely. At some point, I just believe in it because I have chatted with it long enough, in the same way that I could trust a co-author’s implementation without reading the code, just because the results agree and the feedback I get is coherent.

When proofreading this post with Claude, it pointed out that it would not necessarily have flagged it: the prompt explicitly allows the proofs to differ from the paper’s, and some do. For instance, instead of the max over records in the affine risk, the Lean version introduces an extra variable M that bounds every ratio w_i/\varepsilon^{(i)}, and some equalities of infima with public records are proved by approximation. These are technical reformulations rather than different core ideas, but no one has systematically compared the proof routes either.

What next?

Should we have a full differential privacy library in Lean? I think so: it would be a nice way to build on each other’s work and to provide sound definitions. I asked Claude whether we could have used existing libraries, but they sounded ill-fitted:

The main Lean DP library, SampCert (Tristan, de Medeiros et al., Verified Foundations for Differential Privacy, PLDI 2025, arXiv:2412.01671), is built for verified sampling algorithms and powers the DP service of AWS. It works with distributions on countable sets (discrete Laplace and Gaussian samplers) and a single privacy budget. The paper needs continuous Laplace and Gaussian noise, general data distributions, and one budget per record, none of which SampCert covers. So we wrote our own definitions: DP, heterogeneous DP, Rényi divergence, zCDP, and the Laplace distribution. Two consequences:

  • a reader has to check our definitions against the paper; they are not standard, shared objects;
  • none of the surrounding theory (composition, post-processing) comes for free, only what the paper needed.

Where an independent formalization exists, the first drawback can be reduced by proving the two definitions agree. Our Rényi divergence, on which zCDP rests, provably equals the one in Statlib, a Lean library for statistics, for probability measures and every order \alpha > 1 (lean-bridges/renyi-statlib).

But this is a rather anecdotal overlap.

Here I believe we face the problem of the current phase transition: each month, the cost of doing Lean with a new LLM decreases, so investing in old LLM outputs or human outputs seems counterproductive. However, if we consider that generating Lean now has a negligible cost, then what limits it is the design choices made to codify a given field, which I believe should be based on what we consider easiest for humans to follow. It could then be a path towards including Lean code in our submissions, and having reviewers check it more easily than an extremely long LaTeX file generated by an LLM, as we currently ask them to!

Finally, I could not resist the pun in the title. It is also a reference to the book Lean In, which is very controversial from a feminist point of view: most of its advice tends to tell women to adopt the worst of patriarchy to achieve personal success. The analogy with asking mathematicians to adopt new techniques that some also point out as dehumanizing was too sweet for me!
